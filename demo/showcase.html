What this is

Ordinary television in, measured football out.

The input is the same match replay anyone can watch: one moving camera, replays, close‑ups, crowd shots. The pipeline detects and tracks every player, recovers the camera‑to‑pitch geometry frame by frame, puts the ball and the players onto a 105 × 68 m board in metres, spots events, and attaches a name only where the identity evidence is strong enough to survive a gate.

Everything downstream of that — block heights, phases, territory maps, the opponent‑conditioned model, the written report — reads from those coordinates and nothing else. There is no vendor tracking feed, no paid event data, no hand‑annotation of the matches themselves. Where a broadcast cannot support a measurement, the correct output is a refusal, and the system is built to emit one.

The pipeline

Six stages, each with a number attached to it.

  • 1 · video
    Full‑match replays supplied by the user, chunked into ten‑minute segments and registered once in data/matches.yaml. Nothing in the codebase hard‑codes a path to a match.
  • 2 · detect + track
    Player detection and multi‑object tracking, then a jersey‑number layer and a re‑identification relink on top. GS‑HOTA 14.76 → 22.85 on the public SoccerNet‑GSR validation split, 58 sequences, scored by the official evaluator — not by us.
  • 3 · pitch metres
    A homography is fitted per frame and rejected when its residual exceeds 1 m, so a player only gets a coordinate when the geometry is trustworthy. 26.4% of the whole broadcast yields usable geometry on a uniform 1‑in‑5 frame grid — and 80.5% of live wide‑camera frames. The gap between those two numbers is the entire honesty problem of this project.
  • 4 · ball + events
    Ball detection, then a linking pass that is the only coverage number we allow ourselves to quote: 32–52% post‑link usable ball track across the twelve matches. On top of it, pass events (0.97–1.09× official totals on 10 of 12 matches) and goals (100% recall, 81.3% precision).
  • 5 · identity
    Jersey numbers read from close‑up crops, propagated across re‑ID merge groups, checked against the team sheet. It works, and it does not work often enough: 1.0–4.2% of United’s attributed passes carry a named player. That measurement is why there are no per‑player statistics anywhere in this project.
  • 6 · gated report
    Team‑level metrics assembled into a fact store, narrated, and passed through a numeric guardrail that refuses any sentence carrying a number the fact store does not contain. Claims live in a version‑controlled knowledge base with a status field, so a retraction is a state change, not a deletion.

The clip · Fulham v Manchester United, 26 January 2025, second half

This is not a highlights package. It is every passage of a half of football that runs at least eighteen seconds and clears the tracking gate, in order, with a card at each jump naming exactly how much football was left out and why. Why this passage, and not a goal

The obvious choice was a goal, so that is where we started — and the scan came back against the premise. We took every Goal peak the event detector emits at its documented operating point across the corpus (30 peaks in 10 matches) and scored the best window of 120, 90, 60 and 30 seconds ending at each one against the shipped quality gate: ≥ 85% of frames yielding trusted geometry, ≥ 12 tracked players per frame, ≥ 60% ball coverage, both teams on screen.

112 windows scored. Zero cleared the gate — and all 112 failed on the same clause, geometry, never on players or ball. The best two‑minute window at any goal in the corpus reaches 50.9% frame coverage against a required 85%; the best ninety‑second window 48.6%, the best sixty 59.5%, the best thirty 79.5%. A two‑minute continuous tracked passage around a goal does not exist in this footage.

The reason is not a weakness of the tracker. It is what a broadcast is: a goal is immediately followed by a replay, a close‑up of the scorer, the bench, the crowd — and every one of those cuts destroys the pitch geometry that the measurement rests on. Goals are the least measurable moments in a football broadcast, which is an inconvenient thing to discover when you are building a demo and a useful thing to know if you are buying one.

So we picked on measured quality instead, and let the passage be a passage rather than a goal. Fulham v Man Utd carries the highest post‑link ball coverage in the corpus — 0.561 of sampled frames against a corpus median of 0.465 — and its second half yields six gate‑passing passages of 18 seconds or more, 122 seconds in total. One of them ends twenty‑four seconds before the only goal peak of the entire match, which is as close to a goal as this material honestly gets.

What happens in it

Craven Cottage, second half, and this is a half of football in which nobody wants to hold the ball anywhere interesting. Watch United at 10:36. They are playing out from their own goal line and they never leave the touchline: the ball travels from 3 m to 24 m up the pitch and from y = 3 m to y = 2 m across it — two metres of a sixty‑eight metre pitch, hugging the whitewash the whole way. Ninety‑six per cent of the possession, and the only man on the field the identity layer is willing to name is Joshua Zirkzee. It is less build‑up than escape.

At 18:20 Fulham do the thing that fills a highlight reel and no scoresheet. Ninety‑four per cent of the ball, Calvin Bassey the one name the identity gate lets onto the board, and the ball swung the entire width of the pitch — y 61 m to y 1 m — touching 64 m of advancement at its furthest and finishing the passage at 30 m. Nineteen metres backwards from where it started, in front of a block reading low. Possession as pastime.

Then 29:21, and the game finally has a pulse. It is the densest passage of the half — 17.3 tracked players a frame, both teams on screen 88% of the time, the ball contested and neither side holding it for long, the defensive line pushed up to a high read. Twenty‑four seconds after this passage ends, the goal detector puts up the only peak it produces in the entire match: 0.847, second half, 30:05. The official record for this fixture has exactly one goal, in the second half, to Manchester United.

And we do not show you it. The goal is on the other side of a cut. The moment the ball goes in, the camera goes to the celebration and the pitch geometry dies, so the gate refuses the passage and the clip jumps to a card instead. That is the honest output, and it is the same output the system would give a coach: we cannot measure this, here is how far short it fell.

The three marker states, in plain words

Observed

A solid disc. The detector found this player in this frame and a trusted homography put him on the board in metres. This is the only state that is a measurement.

Imputed

A diamond inside two soft rings. We could not see him; a frozen model says he is probably about here, and the rings are its own claimed 50% and 90% regions. On real broadcast footage those rings actually contain the player 21.2% and 55.4% of the time. Read the haze as “roughly here”, never as a probability.

Abstained

A faded cross with a countdown. The model declined: the region it would have had to draw was wider than the threshold it was frozen with, so instead of a guess you get the last place he was actually seen and how long ago that was.

Ball, and names

A filled ball marker means detected in this frame; a hollow ring means carried from a nearby frame. A player’s name appears only where at least three close‑up jersey reads agree and the name is on the team sheet. Everyone else keeps a role band.

Found

Four results. Three of them delete a claim — two of ours, one of the field’s.

1 · The opponent control that dissolved the identity

United build through the middle and finish down the sides: 46.9% of their own‑third ball time is central, 23.9% of their final‑third ball time is. The swing repeats in 11 of 12 matches (sign test p = 0.0063). It looks exactly like a team identity.

Then we ran the same measurement on the opposition in the same twelve broadcasts. They do it in 12 of 12 (p = 0.0005), and finish marginally wider than United do (22.6% central against 23.9%). It is Premier League geometry, not a Manchester United trait. The same control killed the “rigid block” claim (compactness 6.17 ± 0.39 m for United, 5.93 ± 0.29 m for the opponents, F = 1.81, p = 0.34) and the favoured‑flank claim (5 of 12 lean left, p = 0.7744).

player‑004, player‑005, block‑004 · results/MANUTD_IDENTITY_PROFILE.md

2 · Informative censoring inflates the naive rate ~2.5×

Counter‑pressing success looks encouraging if you count only the cases you can decide: among 653 decided turnovers in our corpus, 88.4% end in a regain. The published human‑labelled rate on a 97‑match corpus is 30.2–36.2%.

The gap is not skill, it is the censoring mechanism. The event that breaks the ball track — the ball leaving the frame, going long, being cleared — is exactly the event that implies no regain. So the decided subset over‑represents successes by roughly two and a half times. Any pipeline that reports a naive recovery rate off broadcast tracking is reporting this artifact. The label needs a survival estimator with a stated censoring mechanism, and we say so instead of publishing the 88.4%.

w1‑003 · results/W1B_WINDOW_AND_SAMPLING.md#1

3 · Re‑appearance as free ground truth

When a tracked player goes off camera and the tracker later re‑associates the same id, the position he re‑appears at is ground truth for what an imputation model should have predicted while he was hidden — free, unlabelled, and on the real footage rather than a simulation.

Every comparable model in this literature — DeepMind’s Graph Imputer, a 2026 arXiv preprint on the same problem, and our own frozen model — is calibrated and evaluated on simulated censoring only. Applying this check is, as far as our reading goes, the first calibration test of an off‑screen imputation model on real broadcast footage. Its first result was to demolish our own claim, which is the strongest evidence that the check works.

b4‑019 · results/CALIBRATION_REPLICATION.md · results/PAPER_READ_SCOOP_CHECK.md

4 · Goals are the least measurable moments in a broadcast

Found while building the clip on this page, and reported because it went against the plan. Across 30 detected goals in 10 matches, none of 112 candidate windows of 120, 90, 60 or 30 seconds ending at a goal clears the tracking gate — every one of them fails on pitch geometry, never on player count or ball coverage.

The implication is practical: any product that shows you a confident two‑minute tactical animation of a goal, built from a single broadcast feed, is showing you far more model than measurement. The measurable football is in the quiet passages between the highlights, which is exactly the football nobody clips.

How to check any sentence on this page.

The clip

python tools/tactical_clip.py --goal-scan --match <comma-separated ids>
python tools/tactical_clip.py --match fulham_manutd --half h2 --islands
python tools/tactical_clip.py --match fulham_manutd --half h2 --stitch --stride 2 --crf 23

The first reproduces the 112‑window goal scan; the second prints the six passages and their measured quality and writes them to CSV; the third renders exactly what is embedded above, including the cards. The self‑check asserts, among other things, that the window scoring used to select a passage is the same arithmetic used to refuse one, and that the measured coverage numbers appear in the on‑screen legend.

The standing rule. No metric ships without a validator: an official statistic, a public benchmark with its own evaluator, a pre‑registered bar, or annotated ground truth. When end‑to‑end verification fails, the claim is retracted loudly and the retraction stays in the record. Twelve of the 102 claims on file are retractions of our own work.
